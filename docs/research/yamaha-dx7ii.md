# The Complete DX7II (Yamaha DX7II)

Source: `docs/untracked/The Complete DX7II.pdf`. Howard Massey, *The Complete
DX7II*, Amsco Publications, a division of Music Sales Corporation, New York
(untracked local scan)
Version/date on the document: "Copyright © 1987 by Amsco Publications";
ISBN 0.8256.1119.9 (p. 2)
Read: not exhaustively. This is a 403-page scan with no text layer, so it was
read as page images. Coverage: the front matter and contents in full, then all
sixteen chapters and appendices A-F read by working through them section by
section rather than page by page, roughly one page in three, chosen to cover
every parameter-defining and concept-defining section, plus appendix A's
parameter tables in full. The exercise-only pages (the book has 90 numbered
hands-on exercises, many of them repeating a procedure with different values)
were sampled rather than read individually. Every claim below carries the page
it came from. Note the scan's PDF page numbers run two ahead of the printed
page numbers; all citations here are the **printed** page numbers.

## What it is

Not a manufacturer manual: a 1987 third-party book teaching FM synthesis using
the Yamaha DX7II as the instrument. Six operators per voice, 32 fixed
algorithms, sixteen tone generators, two voices playable in single, dual or
split mode. Its value to us is the *pedagogy*: it explains why each parameter
exists, which is what a manufacturer manual never does. Where the Digitone
manual gives us Elektron's simplified four-operator FM
(`elektron-digitone.md`, p. 84-89), this gives us the full six-operator model it
descends from, plus the reasoning.

## Overlap

**FM operators, carriers and modulators.** The book's framing is three "Cardinal
Rules": changing a carrier's output level changes *volume*; changing a
modulator's output level changes *timbre* (p. 45 ff.). Our engine has no FM
block, so there is no direct overlap, but the Digitone's C / A / B operator
grouping is a four-operator simplification of exactly this.

**Envelopes.** Four rates and four levels, tied to key-on and key-off flags
(p. 145 ff.). Our `Envelope` is ADSR with an attack curve. Theirs is a
rate/level pair model, which is a different and more general shape: see Gaps.

**LFO.** One per voice with wave, speed, delay, mode, pitch-mod depth,
amplitude-mod depth and sync (p. 181-204). Our `LfoBlock` has sync, frequency,
division, waveform, offset, amount, phase.

**Portamento and glissando.** Mode, step and time, where step quantises the
glide "in musical intervals ranging from a semitone up to a full octave. This
yields an effect more commonly known as *glissando*" (p. 271). The Digitone and
Digitakt II both carry the same glide/glissando switch forty years later.

**Unison detune.** Range 0-7, "7 means maximum difference - about a quarter
tone, just fast enough to induce rapid beating effects" (p. 266). Peak's
`UniDeTune` is 0-127 over presumably a similar span
(`novation-peak.md`, p. 28).

## Gaps

**Rate/level envelopes instead of ADSR.** Four levels and four rates, with the
crucial property stated plainly: "the EG rates are absolute speeds and not
absolute times: an R2 of 50 will mean different things, depending upon where L1
is and where L2 is" (p. 349). And the levels are not constrained to rise then
fall, so L4 can sit anywhere. Two consequences the book draws out:

- The envelope can end at a non-zero level, and can be shaped to do things ADSR
  cannot express at all.
- Because rates are speeds, reducing an operator's nominal output level makes
  its envelope *travel faster*: "as the envelope 'squeezes down', it takes less
  time to get from level to level" (p. 389). Our ADSR times are absolute; theirs
  are coupled to level, which is why level scaling also rescales envelope speed
  (p. 247).

For our `Envelope`, a rate/level model is strictly more expressive and no harder
to implement. It is also what the Digitone's operator envelopes are
(attack, decay, *end level*).

**Delayed attack from adjacent low levels, and the deliberate removal of it.**
On the DX7, "this effect can be generated by using adjacent level values which
are both below 20 but equal to one another. In that case, the rate of movement
between these two levels - a rate of movement which is normally meaningless when
traveling between two levels which are the same - acts as a digital delay line."
The DX7II "repaired" this, requiring at least one increment of difference
(p. 389), which silently broke DX7 presets that relied on it. A good example of
a behaviour that was an accident, became an idiom, and then got fixed into a
compatibility break.

**Keyboard level scaling: geographic control of output level.** A break point
note, then a curve (linear or exponential, positive or negative) and a depth
0-99 for each side (p. 229-236). Two things we have no equivalent of:

- It is "the *only* output level control in the DX7II which will allow you to
  increase an operator's output level beyond its maximum set level" (p. 229).
- Applied to modulators rather than carriers, it changes *timbre* across the
  keyboard rather than volume, which is how the book builds a three-way split
  (bass / vibes / brass) inside one voice with one carrier (p. 260).

The manual is also honest about the mechanism: the curves "are actually not true
curves since they alter output level only every three half-steps" (p. 229).

**Fractional keyboard level scaling: a per-three-semitone level table.** The
DX7II's addition. Forty groups of three semitones from C-2 to G8, each holding a
value 0-255, plus a global offset -127 to +127 (p. 248-249). The relationship
between the two scales is stated precisely: "a fractional output level of 255 is
*no greater* than a normal output level of 99... There is simply a finer
gradation to work with" (p. 249), and a negative offset attenuates everything
proportionally. Effectively a drawable per-key level curve, replacing preset
curve shapes with data. Same idea as the Octatrack's LFO designer
(`elektron-octatrack-mkii.md`, p. 61) applied to a different axis.

**Keyboard rate scaling.** A separate control (Rs, 0-7) making envelope rates
faster for higher notes, because "in most acoustic instruments, higher notes
undergo their changes over shorter periods of time than do lower notes"
(p. 351). We have `keyTrack` for filter cutoff and nothing for envelope speed.

**Two sensitivity dimensions that are not the same thing.** `Ams` (amplitude
modulation sensitivity, 0-7) is *per operator* and decides which operators
respond to LFO amplitude modulation. `Pms` (pitch modulation sensitivity, 0-7)
is *not* operator-specific despite living on an operator-specific page, and
"determines how sensitive all six operators will be to *any* kind of pitch
modulation" (p. 187). The book flags the inconsistency itself: "It seems
strange... Confusing? I agree."

**Direct versus controlled modulation as separate depth controls.** `Pmd` is
"used *only* when you wish to set up a direct pitch modulation"; route the LFO
through a real-time controller instead and Pmd goes to 0 while the controller's
own Pmod value takes over (p. 187). And "when you are setting up controlled
modulation routings... the LFO Delay parameter has no effect whatsoever"
(p. 192), because you are the delay. Two depth controls for one destination,
distinguished by who is driving.

**EG bias modulation: real-time control of the EG-to-amplifier signal.** Not the
EG's parameters, and not the LFO: a controller directly attenuates the signal
path between the envelope and the amplifier (p. 209). The stated consequence is
the useful bit: "even if you are completely attenuating the EG bias so that the
operator amplifier is totally shut down, the EG is still going through whatever
movements you programmed in, regardless" (p. 211). So releasing the controller
mid-note reveals the envelope wherever it has got to, rather than restarting it.
And unlike level scaling, "the envelope levels are *not* rescaled" (p. 211), so
it does not change envelope speed.

Five controllers can each drive it with an independent depth 0-99: breath
control, aftertouch, mod wheel, foot control 1, foot control 2, plus a MIDI-in
controller (p. 381-382).

**Micro tuning as a first-class, per-voice feature.** Eleven preset temperaments
(p. 299): equal, pure major, pure minor, mean tone, Pythagorean, Werckmeister,
Kirnberger, Vallotti and Young, 1/4 tone shifted equal, 1/4 tone, 1/8 tone. Plus
user tables. Four details:

- Resolution: "the octave is actually divided up into 1024 increments" rather
  than 1200 cents (p. 299 footnote).
- All presets are anchored on A3 = 440, so "A3 is the only note where the
  equally tempered voice A and the Pure (Major) voice B agree" (p. 304).
- Assignable per voice, so dual mode with the same patch in equal temperament
  and a pure tuning gives beating that "will change from note to note - and in a
  way that is largely unpredictable" (p. 304).
- Beyond microtonal, macrotonal: intervals *larger* than a semitone, creating
  scales with a different number of notes per octave. Appendix E prints Howard
  Sandroff's equally-tempered fifteen-tone scale as a table of fine values
  (p. 393).

Peak has tuning tables at finer resolution (`novation-peak.md`, p. 36) but not
this list of historical temperaments, and not the two-voices-in-two-tunings
trick.

**Random pitch.** Depth 0-7, where "the maximum Depth of 7 will result in the
pitch being shifted by nearly a semitone. For most acoustic instrument
simulations, however, a depth of 1 or 2 is most realistic" (p. 278). Per-note
pitch jitter, the same family as Peak's `Diverge` but triggered per note rather
than fixed per voice.

**Pan EG: a fourth envelope dedicated to stereo position.** Four rates and four
levels, "exactly the same as those of the operator EGs or the pitch EG", where
50 is centre, below 50 shifts to output A and above 50 to output B (p. 317). Pan
has four modes and three additional sources: LFO, velocity, or note number
(p. 314-317). Note number panning gives keyboard-position stereo automatically,
and the book points out it can be used as a positional crossfade between two
voices in dual mode (p. 351). Our `StereoPanner` takes a static value.

Also worth noting: pan is monophonic. "all the voices will be repositioned or
have their output levels adjusted simultaneously, according to the latest
information received" (p. 317), which the book reframes as a feature: playing
rapid chords makes the whole sound jump around pseudo-randomly.

**Pitch EG with a range parameter.** Range is 8 octaves by default but settable
to 1 or 2, and because rates are speeds, narrowing the range makes the same rate
value move *slower* in absolute time (p. 389). A range control that is also, in
effect, a time control.

**EG forced damping.** When voice stealing occurs, the stolen voice's envelope
normally continues from wherever it was, so the new note skips its attack. Turn
this on and the new note gets its full envelope (p. 297). Voice-stealing
artefacts as a documented switch rather than an unstated behaviour. Compare the
Digitone's voice-stealing menu (`elektron-digitone.md`, p. 36), which chooses
*which* voice to steal but says nothing about what the stolen voice does.

**Four key modes, with polyphony as an explicit cost.** Polyphonic (16 notes),
monophonic, unison poly (4 tone generators per note, so 4-note polyphony), and
unison mono (p. 265-266). The trade is stated as arithmetic rather than hidden.
And dual mode with the same voice twice plus "dual detune" 0-7 gives the same
chorusing "but with eight-note polyphony instead of four" (p. 294), which is a
better deal the book tells you to prefer.

**Pitch bend modes beyond a range.** Range 0-12, step 0-12 (quantising bend to
semitones), and a mode: normal, low, high, or key-on (p. 269-270). `low` bends
only the lowest note in a held chord, `high` only the highest, and `key on`
excludes notes already sustaining so that "these new added notes *are* affected
by the movement of the wheel, while the sustaining chord is not" (p. 271). Three
guitar-idiom behaviours from one enum.

**Compare mode.** Hold a button to hear the stored version of the voice, release
to return to your edits (p. 139-141), the same idea as Peak's Compare
(`novation-peak.md`, p. 11), and worth noting it was standard by 1987.

**EG and scaling copy between operators.** One key copies a whole envelope plus
all level-scaling values from one operator to another (p. 166-167), with the
warning that copying carrier EGs onto modulators and expecting realism is the
common mistake (p. 349).

**The book's own design advice, which is the transferable part.** Three examples:

- On why detune matters: it hides "the digital precision of this instrument" and
  helps "impart a more 'real' quality" (p. 351). Add it "as a last step, and in
  small and careful doses" (p. 351).
- On using R4 as pseudo-reverb: offsetting release rates between operators and
  between the two voices in dual mode "you can essentially create very convincing
  reverb effects" that push the sound back in the near-far axis (p. 353).
- On the whole exercise: "USE YOUR EARS! ... numbers, in and of themselves, are
  meaningless. This is an easy trap to fall into: don't become yet another victim
  of the dread Numbers Game" (p. 353).

## Not worth copying

**Fixed algorithms.** Thirty-two hard-wired operator topologies, chosen from a
chart (p. 349). This is a 1983 ROM constraint. A software FM block should let
the routing be data. The book's own selection heuristics ("how many carriers do
you need? how many modulators, and how should they be plugged into these
carriers?", p. 347) are worth keeping as *documentation*; the fixed menu is not.

**Parameters that only take effect on the next note.** Unison detune "will not
change in real time - you'll need to continuously re-trigger the note in order
to completely 'enter' the new value (though it changes immediately in the LCD,
the microprocessor does not receive the new value until a new note is played)"
(p. 266). Same for the CS slider used for EG bias (p. 292). A UI that shows a
value the engine has not accepted.

**Performance edit parameters that reset whenever you leave performance mode,
and cannot be saved from voice edit mode** (p. 283). The book calls the wider
problem out directly: you cannot swap which voices a performance memory uses
without rebuilding it, "one of the glaring weaknesses of the memory organization
of this instrument" (p. 292).

**Two overlapping level-scaling modes where switching between them is lossy.**
Changing from fractional back to normal "will cause the normal scaling
parameters (and the sound) to be quasi-initialized to a state of 'no-scaling'"
(p. 260). Fractional is the general case; normal is a preset-curve UI on top of
it. Build one, and give it curve presets.

**Ranges expressed as 0-99 because the display is two digits.** Almost every
parameter (p. 379-382). Same objection as the Novation and Elektron 0-127
ranges: the wire format leaking into the model.

**The parameter-per-edit-switch UI** (32 edit switches, each cycling through
several LCD pages). The book needs a two-page appendix just to index it
(p. 379-382).

**Pan being monophonic** (p. 317). A voice-allocation limit reframed as a
happy accident. In software, pan per voice is free.

## Open questions

- The exact shape of the four level-scaling curves. The book gives their names
  (+lin, -lin, +exp, -exp), a graph, and the fact that they step every three
  semitones (p. 229, p. 236), but no formula, and no statement of what the
  exponential base is.
- How depth 0-99 maps onto that curve. "a depth value of 99 will indicate that
  the curve has maximum effect" (p. 236) is the whole specification.
- The relationship between an operator's nominal level and its envelope speed.
  The book states the effect repeatedly ("as the envelope squeezes down, it takes
  less time to get from level to level", p. 389) but never gives the function,
  which matters because it means level and time are not independent.
- Whether `Ams` scales LFO amplitude modulation only, or also the EG bias
  modulation that shares it. The book says EG bias "has nothing whatsoever to do
  with the LFO, even though its associated operator-specific sensitivity control
  is also used by the LFO" (p. 209) without saying whether the two uses scale
  identically.
- How the 1024-increment octave maps to the printed micro tuning values. Appendix
  E's fifteen-tone table lists values like 2116 and gaps of 68 or 69 (p. 393),
  which are neither cents nor 1024ths of an octave directly; the encoding is
  never stated.
- What "multi" LFO mode actually does per voice. The book says the sixteen
  physical LFOs "will each act in a more independent fashion" (p. 183) and
  promises a detailed discussion, but the later treatment (p. 204-207) describes
  the effect without specifying the phase relationship.
- Whether Pan EG runs per note or per voice-mode. Given pan is stated to be
  monophonic (p. 317), it is unclear whether a Pan EG retriggers on every note or
  only on the first of a legato phrase.
